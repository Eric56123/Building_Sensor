Abstract
Earthquakes kill mainly by collapsing occupied housing. Permanent structural monitoring goes
almost exclusively to high-value infrastructure and whether a damaged house can be reoccupied
is settled by inspection on foot, at a rate bounded by inspector-hours. This dissertation asks how
much damage information a single-low cost accelerometer establishes about a multi-storey frame
and whether embedding the equation of motion in a learned inverse model improves on classical
modal analysis of the same measurement. Evidence comes from a three-storey steel shear frame
carrying one consumer-grade accelerometer on a Raspberry Pi, the whole chain running on the
node, with damage introduced by loosening the screws at one of four plates in four graded amounts.

Five teardown and rebuild cycles put the reassembly floors at 0.30, 0.46 and 0.32%, mea-
sured rather than assumed. Severe damage clears them by 52 to 202 times at every location and
an eighth of a turn is resolved at all four. The first-mode shift falls monotonically with grade at
11 of 12 location and mode combinations, but saturates above one turn, so severity is an ordinal
trend and not a classification. Localisation is the strongest positive result: nearest-signature
matching on floor-normalised shifts assigned all twelve replicated severe runs correctly and 18
of 18 at two further sensor positions.

Every route that passes through a parametrised model of the frame fails in the same place. Direct
inversion closes every measured frequency exactly, yet where the problem is square its solutions
require a storey to have stiffened, which loosening a screw cannot do. Re-parametrising by plate
does not repair it and localises at chance. The network inherits the same parametrisation and
reaches 6 of 9 against the classical rule’s of 9 of 9. Its physics term cut the residual it penalises
by 17.8% and moved on task metric beyond fold and seed scatter. Storey stiffness was recovered
at no location. The obstacle is the shear-chain model those routes share rather than the resolution
of the measurement. The output is a change-detection signal: it could prioritise inspection, but it
is not a safety assessment. A semi-rigid connection model with a rotational spring at each plate
is the next family to test, on vectors already recorded.

Chapter 1


Introduction

Earthquakes kill mainly by collapsing occupied housing, yet permanent structural monitoring is
installed almost exclusively on high-value infrastructure, where a per-structure cost of thousands
is proportionate to the asset. After an event, whether a damaged house can be reoccupied is
decided by physical inspection, a process bounded by the number of trained inspectors and the
time each building takes. Instrumentation cheap enough to leave on a house could direct that
effort to the buildings whose dynamic behaviour has changed. Cost per building is the deployment
obstacle. Whether the modelling assumptions survive that constraint is what this dissertation tests.

A sensor at that price must do everything on one node: a single low-cost accelerometer,
processing and inference performed locally, and an output a non-specialist can act on. That raises
a measurement question, of what one accelerometer at one point can establish about damage
and an inference question of whether the stiffness of individual storeys can be recovered from
what it sees. Physics-informed neural networks are increasingly proposed for the second, on the
argument that embedding the governing equation in the training loss supplies information that
sparse data does not. Chapter 2 reviews both literatures and identifies where the evidence is
thin.


1.1     Aims and Objectives
The central question of this dissertation is: how much damage information can a single low-cost
accelerometer establish about a multi-storey frame and does embedding the equation of motion
in a learned inverse model improve on what classical modal analysis achieves from the same
measurement? It is pursued through six objectives:

   1. Establish what a single accelerometer resolves against rebuild variability.


                                                 1
Chapter 1. Introduction                                                                          2

   2. Detect damage introduced at a screwed joint.

   3. Track damage severity across a graded series.

   4. Identify which plate was loosened.

   5. Assess whether per-storey stiffness can be recovered from the measured modal data, and
      where recovery fails, establish why.

   6. Establish whether the physics term in the network’s loss earns its place, by ablation against
      an otherwise identical network.

Evidence comes from a three-storey steel shear frame on a shaking table, instrumented with one
ADXL345 accelerometer on a Raspberry Pi, with damage introduced by loosening the screws
at one of four plates in four graded amounts. Objectives one to four are addressed by classical
modal identification, five and six by a physics-informed network trained on synthetic data and
by direct inversion of the same measured modal vectors. Chapter 2 sets out the literature the
results are judged against; Chapters 3 and 4 describe the methods and report the measurements;
Chapter 5 interprets them and Chapter 6 states what follows.

Background


2.1     Seismic risk in low-rise housing and the assessment
        gap
Between 1980 and 2009, earthquakes caused around 372,600 deaths worldwide and trauma
from building collapse was the leading cause of death (Doocy et al., 2013); secondary hazards
such as landslides and tsunamis can dominate individual disasters but rarely the record as a
whole (Marano et al., 2010). Much of the exposed residential stock is unreinforced masonry
(Figure 2.1). Such buildings fail under lateral loading through in-plane diagonal shear cracking
(Hafner et al., 2023) and through out-of-plane mechanisms, among the most dangerous in
earthquakes, whose activation depends on the strength of connections between walls, floors and
roofs (Destro Bisol et al., 2024). Guidance for safer non-engineered construction (Arya et al.,
2014) acts at construction, not on houses already standing.

Post-earthquake reoccupation is settled by inspection on foot, by trained personnel working to
standardised assessment forms and placards (Applied Technology Council, 1989). The procedure
is systematic; what limits it is inspector-hours: about 75,000 buildings were inspected after
the 2009 L’Aquila earthquake, coordinated by the Italian National Civil Protection Department
using the AeDES form (Zucconi and Sorrentino, 2022), and where capacity is thinner the same
building-by-building procedure is slower, less complete, or displaced onto residents.

Permanent instrumentation could support post-earthquake triage by flagging structures whose
dynamic properties have changed. In practice, such monitoring is concentrated on high-value
assets: bridges, dams and tall buildings (Brownjohn, 2007), with instrumented bridges carrying
on the order of 250 sensors each (Cawley, 2018); costs acceptable for such assets do not scale

2.2      Vibration-based structural health monitoring
Vibration-based monitoring rests on the premise that damage alters the stiffness, mass or energy
dissipation of a structure and so its dynamic response (Farrar and Worden, 2007). Loss of
stiffness lowers natural frequencies, the modal property that can be measured most reliably and
is usually framed as a statistical pattern-recognition problem (Farrar and Worden, 2007; Worden
et al., 2007). Rytter (1993) ordered the task into four levels of increasing difficulty: detecting
that damage exists, locating it, quantifying its severity and predicting remaining life; a fifth level,
classifying damage type, was added later by Worden and Dulieu-Barton (2004). Results here
reach the first two levels, address the third in part and do not attempt the fourth.

2.3      Damage localisation from modal data
Natural frequencies are a global property of a structure, so it is not obvious that shifts in them
can identify anything beyond the existence of damage (Doebling et al., 1996). The classical
route around this is signature matching. Cawley and Adams (1979) observed that the ratio of
frequency shifts between two modes depends on where damage is and is largely independent
of how severe it is, so a measured vector of shift ratios acts as a location signature that can be
matched against a library computed from a model. The nearest-signature rule used here comes
directly from that argument.

Matching is not unique identification. Cracks of different severity at different positions
can produce identical changes in the first few natural frequencies (Morassi and Rollo, 2001), and
symmetric structures return identical signatures for symmetrically placed damage. Mode-shape
methods, curvature (Pandey et al., 1991), modal strain energy (Shi et al., 1998) or modal assurance
criterion (Allemang, 2003) carry genuine spatial information but they require simultaneous
measurement at several points, which a single sensor cannot provide. In a comparative study
on the I-40 bridge, the methods trialled located damage only at the most severe of four levels
(Farrar and Jauregui, 1998). Where matching cannot separate candidate locations, the option left
is to estimate the stiffness parameters directly.

2.4     Model updating and the inverse problem
Recovering stiffness parameters from modal data is an inverse problem (Figure 2.2), normally
posed as sensitivity-based finite element model updating: adjust a numerical model’s parameters
until its predicted modal properties match the measured ones (Mottershead and Friswell, 1993).
The problem is ill-conditioned and non-identifiable where only limited modal information is
observed.

The deeper difficulty is that recovered parameters inherit the assumptions of the forward
model. Where the parametrisation does not correspond to how the structure actually behaves,
updating still returns numbers, but they need not have physical meaning (Friswell, 2007; Simoen
et al., 2015). Noise-driven error shrinks as more data is collected, whereas discrepancy between
model and reality leads to increasing confidence in a wrong parameter value (Brynjarsdóttir
and O’Hagan, 2014). Distinguishing the two matters when a recovered stiffness falls outside its
admissible range.

2.5      Physics-informed machine learning in structural
         dynamics
Physics-informed neural networks embed a governing equation in the training loss, penalising
a network both for mismatching data and for violating the physics (Raissi et al., 2019), as set
out in Figure 2.3. The claimed benefits are data efficiency, better extrapolation, and the ability
to recover unknown parameters alongside the solution in inverse problems (Karniadakis et al.,
2021). Structural dynamics has used it for response prediction (Zhang et al., 2020), system
identification (Lai et al., 2021) and closest to this work, damage location and severity in a shear
frame (Baniya and Maity, 2026).

Whether the physics term earns its place is less settled than the volume of applications suggests.
Chapter 2. Background                                                                                   8




Figure 2.3: The term whose value this dissertation tests. Training penalises the network twice over. One
penalty grows when its predictions disagree with the training labels. The other grows when the stiffnesses
it predicts fail to reproduce the observed modes through the governing equation, a quantity computed from
the network’s predicted stiffness through the eigenproblem, then backpropagated to the weights. A single
weight controls how heavily the second penalty counts, and that weight is the only quantity varied in the
ablation reported in Section 4.6; at a weight of zero the lower branch is switched off and what remains is
an otherwise identical network trained on labels alone. After the formulation of Raissi et al. (2019).


Predictive error depends strongly on the weight given to the physics loss, rebalancing alone
changes accuracy by one or two orders of magnitude and the optimal weights are problem-specific
rather than transferable (Wang et al., 2021). The physics term can also make training harder
because the differential operator it encodes may itself be badly conditioned (Krishnapriyan
et al., 2021). More widely, 79% of studies claiming to beat a standard numerical method used a
weak baseline (McGreivy and Hakim, 2024). A controlled comparison found physics-informed
networks did not outperform the finite element method on accuracy or solution time (Grossmann
et al., 2024). None of this makes physics-informed learning irrelevant; it means the term’s
contribution has to be demonstrated by ablation rather than assumed from the label.

Such ablations exist. Al-Adly and Kripakaran (2024) report improvement as physics con-
straints are added for plate monitoring, on one dataset and without run-to-run scatter; Lenau
et al. (2026) show that single training runs can mislead. What appears uncommon in structural
applications is an ablation of the physics weight replicated across independent datasets with fold
and seed variability reported, the comparison made in this dissertation.

2.7     Summary and research gap
The gap is not the absence of any one method. Modal damage detection, model updating, physics-
informed learning and MEMS sensing are each established. What is missing is evidence on how
far a single low-cost accelerometer can be taken when all four components are constrained to the
same setting: a measured baseline against which shifts are judged, localisation from frequencies
alone, stiffness recovery through a forward model that may not fit, and a physics-informed
network whose physics term is ablated rather than assumed. The objectives of Chapter 1 address
each in turn.

3.4       Modal identification
Modal frequencies are estimated per tap, using the parameters of Table 3.6. Each impulse is
written as a continuous recording divided into contiguous 4.0 s windows; the windows of a tap
are concatenated, transformed to a power spectral density by Welch’s method (Welch, 1967),
and the peaks refined to sub-bin precision by a parabola fitted to the log spectrum across the
peak bin and its two neighbours. The whole record is used rather than the window containing
the impulse, for resolution. Baseline and damaged captures share a record length within every
comparison except the Floor 2 and Floor 3 cells of the graded series, where 32 s damage captures
are compared against a 20 s baseline; the consequence is contained in the per-tap scatter of
Section 4.3.1.

3.5      Analysis of the measured modal shifts

3.5.1     Reassembly repeatability floors
The frame is a screwed assembly and rebuilding it changes its frequencies whether or not damage
has been introduced, so every shift is judged against measured rebuild variability rather than
against zero. Five complete dismantle-and-rebuild cycles of the undamaged frame give, for each
mode, a standard deviation of the modal frequency as a percentage of its mean; twice that value
Chapter 3. Methodology                                                                                  19

is the operational reassembly floor for that mode. With five cycles, it is an operational threshold
rather than a formal exclusion limit. Every shift is compared against the floor for its own mode,
which matters because the three floors measured in Section 4.1.4 differ by more than 50%.


3.5.2     Detection
For the severe-damage comparison, the statistic is the first-mode shift and the detection margin is
the ratio |Δ 𝑓1 |/floor( 𝑓1 ). For the graded series, detectability is reported mode by mode because
a light grade at an upper storey may move a higher mode more than the fundamental. Detection
is reported as a margin rather than a p-value; where a classification result is tested instead,
significance comes from permutation of the labels and a binomial bound is not used. Table 3.7
states why. Severity is read as an ordinal scale, with monotonicity reported per location and
per mode and the number of location-mode combinations at which it holds stated rather than
asserted globally.

3.6.3    The sampled-stiffness four-storey dataset
The benchmark data has a defect that no amount of training repairs, and it falls on the physics
term specifically. Its published stiffness matrices do not lie in the span of Equation 3.7: the
fractions derived from them reconstruct those matrices to between 0.7% and 5.1% relative
error, so the physics residual at the true fractions is of order 10−3 rather than zero and on the
mild-damage patterns, that residual is smaller at the intact state than at the truth. The physics
term therefore scores a wrong answer above the right one, and a weight ablation on that data
cannot measure what the regulariser does.

A second dataset was generated to remove both the pattern shortage and the representation error
at source (Table 3.9). Stiffness fractions are sampled rather than derived, and the stiffness matrix
is built from the sampled vector by the same superposition the physics loss assumes, so the true
fractions are exact and the residual falls to order 10−8 . Everything downstream is unchanged, so
the two datasets only differ in how the damage states were chosen.


3.6.4    What the four-storey stage established
Three findings determined that a retargeted model was required before the rig comparison could
be run.

The physics weight does not change held-out performance. On the benchmark patterns, the
ablation left localisation at or below the 0.25 chance rate, a result that is uninformative for the
reason given in Section 3.6.3. On the sampled-stiffness data the ablation is well posed and the
answer is the same (Table 4.11.

Representation error on the benchmark is carried forward as a limitation rather than repaired.

Architectural mismatch with the rig. The architecture assumes four simultaneous chan-
nels, one per floor. The rig carries one accelerometer, so a four-channel input can only be formed
by replicating a single measurement, which supplies no information the network can use to
distinguish storeys.

3.7     Direct inversion of the measured modal vectors
To separate the network’s contribution from that of the forward model it assumes, the same
measured modal vectors are also inverted directly through that model, with no network involved.
For each damaged case, the three stiffness fractions are fitted by least squares, so that the
eigenfrequencies of K(k) reproduce the measured frequencies. The fit is unconstrained rather
than bounded to 𝑘 𝑖 ≤ 1, so a case requiring an inadmissible stiffness is visible as such rather
than driven to the boundary.

A single-start least squares solve can converge to a rank-deficient stationary point rather
than to a root, returning a large residual without establishing that no exact fit exists. The solution
set is therefore enumerated by multi-start rather than taken from one optimisation, under the
settings of Table 3.11. A branch is admissible where every 𝑘 𝑖 ≤ 1, since loosening a screwed
joint cannot stiffen a storey. Two further quantities are computed at each branch: the condition
number of the Jacobian and the proportion of perturbed draws returning any 𝑘 1 > 1, which
turns an inadmissible point estimate into a statement about whether inadmissibility survives
measurement uncertainty. Floor 3 is excluded from the enumeration. Its 𝑓2 is voided by the
second harmonic of 𝑓1 , leaving two measured frequencies against three unknowns, so the square
problem is not posed and no branch structure exists to enumerate.


3.7.1    Plate-hypothesis inversion
The storey parametrisation cannot express damage at a plate, which connects the columns above
and below it. A second inversion tests the plate parametrisation directly. Four one-parameter
hypotheses are posed, each softening the two storeys its plate adjoins by a common factor 𝑑,
Chapter 3. Methodology                                                                                            31

the base plate (𝑑, 1, 1), Floor 1 as (𝑑, 𝑑, 1) Floor 2 as (1, 𝑑, 𝑑) and Floor 3 as (1, 1, 𝑑). Each
is fitted by least squares in the single parameter 𝑑 and ranked by residual, the smallest giving
the predicted location. These fits are over-determined, three measured frequencies to one free
parameter, so unlike the three-parameter inversion they are immune to non-uniqueness. A
two-parameter variant is also reported but its ranking is not interpretable: the two-parameter
Floor 1 and Floor 2 hypotheses nest the one-parameter base-plate and Floor 3 hypotheses as
special cases, so their residuals cannot be larger and a comparison across the four is a comparison
between models of different flexibility. The invalidity is reported rather than their ranking.

4.6.7      Difference between the synthetic and experimental localisation
           tasks
The synthetic task labels each sample with the storey carrying the greatest stiffness reduction;
the experimental task identifies the loosened plate. Each plate connects the columns above and
below it, so the two labels are not equivalent and the 93.9% synthetic accuracy does not transfer.
The training set also draws damaged storeys independently, where loosening one plate changes
two adjacent storeys together.

Discussion

The experimental campaign and the network were driven by the same underlying measurement,
the set of natural frequencies identified from one accelerometer, so the two sets of results can be
compared on identical inputs, subject to the reproducibility caveat of Section 5.5. Comparing
them produces one finding rather than several. The three routes that pass through a parametrised
model of the frame, the direct inversion, the network and the physics term in its loss, all fail to
recover physically meaningful storey stiffness and they fail in the same place. The one route
that applies no forward model identified the loosened plate in all twelve replicated runs. For
the damage states and controlled conditions tested here, non-identifiability is present but not
decisive: even the exact branches are inadmissible or place the softening on the wrong storey.
The decisive limit is the model those three routes share. Table 5.1 records the position reached
against each objective of Table 3.1.

5.1      What a single low-cost accelerometer resolves
Measuring the reassembly floor, rather than assuming one, is what makes the rest of the
measurements interpretable: any shift smaller than the movement a rebuild produces cannot be
attributed (Section 3.5.1. Five teardown and rebuild cycles put the floor at the values of Table
4.3, and every margin below is a multiple of the floor for its own mode. Those values are one to
two orders of magnitude tighter than the variability reported for structures in service (Sohn et al.,
1999; Peeters and De Roeck, 2001) and the comparison does not favour the present work. A
reassembly floor measured without thermal variation bounds the nuisance that dominates this
Chapter 5. Discussion                                                                              59

structure, not the one that would dominate a deployed installation.

Against that floor, severe damage is unambiguous, clearing it by 52 to 202 times, with the
fundamental shifting from the Session 4 baseline of 2.94 Hz to 1.16 Hz for Floor 1 damage.
These are large changes by the standards of the monitoring literature, where a crack removing
one per cent of a section changes a resonant frequency by roughly 0.1% (Cawley, 2018), so the
experiment sits at the severe end of the damage scale rather than the incipient end where detection
is difficult. The trace grade is the more informative test. An eighth of a turn is resolved on the
first mode at three of the four locations, and at Floor 3 only on the third (Section 4.3.2). The fun-
damental is least sensitive to damage at the top of the structure, where the storey it softens carries
the smallest share of that mode’s strain energy, so no single mode suffices across the four locations.

Severity behaves as an ordinal quantity rather than a measurable one. The first-mode shift falls
monotonically with grade at all four locations and the trend holds at 11 of 12 location and mode
combinations but it saturates above one turn, moving 9.8% at the base between moderate and
severe against 1.2% at Floor 2. This is consistent with progressive loss of clamping constraint,
after which further loosening produces diminishing change in effective stiffness, though the
mechanism was not measured directly. Each cell below the severe grade is a single damage
application, so this is a trend and not a demonstrated classification. In the terms of Rytter (1993),
the first level is reached comfortably and the third in part; remaining-life prognosis was outside
the scope of the design.


5.2     Why empirical signature matching succeeded without a
        forward model
The nearest-signature rule originates from Cawley and Adams (1979), whose argument is set out
in Section 4.4. The adaptation made here is to normalise each component of the modal shift
vector by its own measured floor, which makes the components commensurate and each margin
readable as a multiple of the variability the structure generates unaided. Under leave-one-out
cross-validation, the rule assigned every replicated severe run correctly, under the per-run
convention and in both fixed signature spaces. The closest pair of class means is separated by 33
floor-units against a largest replicate standard deviation of 1.7 (Section 4.4.1). Repeating the
damage with the accelerometer on Floor 2 and Floor 1 gave 18 of 18 correct against references
recorded at a different position from the runs being tested.

As the runs are clustered by location and by damage cycle, the result is tested by permut-
ing the location labels rather than by a binomial interval. Over 10,000 shuffles none reached
Chapter 5. Discussion                                                                           60

a perfect score under the per-run convention or in the two-mode space, and 41 did so in the
three-mode space, giving p = 0.0001, 0.0001 and 0.0042. The first two sit at the resolution floor
of 1/(𝑁 + 1), so they are separable from chance but not from each other.

Set against published localisation from modal data this is a respectable result and easy to
overstate. On the I-40 bridge, the methods trialled located damage only at the most severe of four
induced levels, using a full sensor array (Farrar and Jauregui, 1998); the present work reaches the
same level of the Rytter hierarchy, on a much simpler structure and under controlled excitation.
Correct classification is also not unique identification, since damage of different severity at
different positions can produce identical changes in the first few frequencies (Morassi and Rollo,
2001), and the rule selects among four known candidates without excluding an unmodelled fifth.

The base plate against Floor 1 pair is where three modes earn their place. On the first
mode, the two are 1.8 percentage points apart, six times its floor since both soften the bottom of
the structure and the fundamental sees them as nearly the same event; on the third they are 8.3
points apart, 25.9 times its floor. Curvature and modal strain-energy methods would separate this
pair without difficulty (Pandey et al., 1991; Shi et al., 1998) but need simultaneous measurement
at several points, which a single sensor cannot provide. Three frequencies measured at one
point retained enough spatial information to make a distinction the fundamental could not. The
placement result matters most for deployment: damage-induced shifts agree across the three
sensor positions to within 1 to 2% while the relative modal amplitudes change by an order of
magnitude. What the sensor sees depends strongly on where it is; what the signature encodes
does not, provided the baseline is recorded at the same position, though a position could in
principle be chosen at which a mode is not recoverable at all. The second level of Rytter (1993)
is reached, and the fourth was not attempted.


5.3     Why the model-based routes failed and what kind of
        failure it is
Three routes pass through the same parametrised model of the frame: the direct inversion, the
network and the physics term in the network’s loss. All three underperform, and the inversion is
the one to take first because it removes the network as a variable.


5.3.1    The failure appears before any network is involved
Inverting the measured modal vectors directly through the three-storey shear model isolates the
model from everything the network adds. With three resolved modes and three-storey stiffnesses
the system is dimensionally determined, though not unique, well conditioned or physically valid,
Chapter 5. Discussion                                                                              61

a caution already mentioned in Section 3.7.

Enumerating the solution set settles the first of those. The inversion returns four distinct
exact branches at the base plate, two at Floor 1 and four at Floor 2, so it is non-identifiable in the
sense of Katafygiotis and Beck (1998) and reporting one recovered vector per case understates
the problem.

Non-identifiability is not what limits stiffness recovery here because almost none of the
branches are usable. At the base plate all four exact solutions carry a fraction above unity and
loosening a screwed joint cannot stiffen a storey. The Floor 1 branches, (1.285, 0.059, 1.415)
and (2.861, 0.058, 0.646) place the first storey (in the first branch the third as well) above unity
while reducing the middle one almost to nothing. One of the four Floor 2 branches is admissible,
and it places most of the softening on storey 1 at 𝑘 1 = 0.271, which does not adjoin the plate
that was loosened. At Floor 3 the enumeration is not posed at all: 𝑓2 is voided by the second
harmonic of 𝑓1 , leaving two modes against three unknowns, so no enumeration was run and any
solution set is a one-parameter family. Across the locations where the problem is square, the
model’s entire exact-solution set lies outside the region the experiment can have produced.

Neither measurement error nor conditioning accounts for this. Perturbing the measured
frequencies by their own reassembly standard deviations returns a fraction above unity in every
draw at the base plate and Floor 1 and none at Floor 2, at condition numbers of 2.38 and 1.74,
while the branches themselves close all three measured frequencies to better than 6 × 10−14 Hz
and remain physically impossible. A failure driven by noise or poor conditioning would be
expected to yield a better answer for a better fit.

This is the behaviour Friswell (2007) and Simoen et al. (2015) describe, in which recov-
ered parameters inherit the assumptions of the forward model and need not carry physical
meaning and it matches the distinction Brynjarsdóttir and O’Hagan (2014) draw between two
kinds of error: noise-driven error shrinks as data accumulates, whereas discrepancy between
model and reality produces increasing confidence in a wrong value. A fit this exact to a physically
impossible answer is the signature of discrepancy rather than noise.


5.3.2    The geometric hypothesis, tested
A plate connects the columns above and below it, so loosening one softens two storeys together,

while the forward model builds the stiffness matrix as 𝐾 (𝑘) = 𝑖 𝑘 𝑖 𝐾𝑖 , a sum in which each
storey is scaled on its own. That suggests an obvious repair: parametrise by plate rather than by
storey, so each damage variable softens the two storeys its plate adjoins.
Chapter 5. Discussion                                                                                62



The repair does not work and it fails in two ways at once. Taking the lowest residual as
the localisation recovers the correct plate in one of four cases, the chance rate. The ranking
is weaker even than that count suggests, since only the correct call is decisive, separated from
its runner-up by 0.795 Hz, while the other three separate by 0.25 Hz or less. Perturbing the
measured frequencies leaves every ranking intact, so the three incorrect calls are stable rather
than artefacts of scatter. Nor does any hypothesis fit: the winning residuals of 0.13 to 0.56 Hz
stand twelve orders of magnitude above the exact branches of the storey model, against modes
lying between 1 and 12 Hz.

The two parametrisations therefore fail in complementary ways and that is what locates
the problem. The storey model is square, reproduces the measurements exactly and can only
do so from outside the admissible region. The plate model is over-determined, is immune
to the non-uniqueness above for that reason and cannot reproduce the measurements at all.
Neither shear-chain parametrisation tested here represents the damage the rig applies. The limit
therefore sits with the linear shear-chain family rather than with the choice of parametrisation
inside it. Two further observations point the same way. Damping ratios on the undamaged
frame span 6.8, 0.7 and 0.2% across the three modes, more than an order of magnitude and
beyond what a proportional-damping model can represent, against 1% assumed at the four-storey
stage; and the tap-to-tap behaviour of Section 5.4 is not that of a linear time-invariant system at all.

The model fitting the undamaged frame is not evidence against this. Three free stiffnesses fitted
to three measured frequencies must fit by parameter counting alone, which is why Section 3.6.5
records a residual of order 10−15 Hz as confirming convergence rather than establishing model
quality. Independent evidence of the same shortfall comes from the four-storey benchmark,
whose published stiffness matrices lie outside the span of that assembly equation at 0.7 to 5.1%
reconstruction error.


5.3.3    The network inherits, and what it does not
The network is trained on damage generated as pure storey-stiffness reduction, so it inherits that
parametrisation in full. One of its five archived predictions has to be set aside before the rest can
be read. Section 4.6.3 records that two modes were unmeasurable in the Floor 3 capture, and that
substituting unchanged ratios for them reproduces the archived vector to within 0.005. A vector
in which only 𝑓1 has moved is by construction the signature of storey 1 damage, so that call was
imposed by the substitution rather than learned. Its detection call stands, since 𝑓1 was measured
and is the component that carries the severity index past the threshold, but its localisation does not.
Chapter 5. Discussion                                                                           63

On measured inputs the pattern is different and it is stable. Across the seed sweep of Ta-
ble 4.14, the network localises two of the three storey cases and fails at Floor 2 alone, reaching
the middle storey at Floor 2 trace and only in two or three seeds of twenty. Scored per run, the
network reaches 6 of 9 across the graded cells, against the classical rule’s 9 of 9 on the severe
replicates (Table 4.18).

This is not an accident of which cases were tested. The model can place damage at the
bottom or the top of the structure and nowhere between, and Floor 2 is the one location whose
plate would require the middle storey to be named. This is the degeneracy of Section 5.3.1
arriving by a second route: the middle storey is the one a triple of natural frequencies constrains
least, and it is where both the inversion and the network give way.

The base plate has no parameter in the model, so no correct answer exists for it inside
the label space. The network returns a confident call regardless, predicting (0.066, 0.924, 0.957)
and placing the minimum on 𝑘 1 in all forty runs. Storey 1 adjoins the base plate, so this is the
least wrong of the three available answers without being a right one.

The one alternative decision rule tested does not rescue the predictions. Scoring the same
predicted vectors by nearest signature rather than by smallest fraction changes nothing on the
replicated severe captures, where the two rules agree on every item of all forty runs and makes
matters worse across the graded cells, losing on thirteen of twenty paired seeds at the higher
weight. Whatever the argmin discards, nearest signature does not recover. What the network
reports is a statement about what it can represent and not about how its output is read and Section
4.6.7 establishes separately that the storey it names and the plate the experiment loosened are not
the same label.


5.3.4    Why the physics loss earned nothing
The physics term belongs to the same argument. The eigenvalue residual asks that the predicted
stiffnesses reproduce the observed modes through the same forward map that generated the
labels, so on supervised synthetic data it restates information the supervision already carries.
Setting 𝜆 = 1.0 on the sampled-stiffness data reduces the converged physics residual by 17.8%
while every task metric moves by less than the fold and seed scatter (Section 4.6). The same
comparison on the measured rig predictions moves localisation accuracy by 0.004 at 𝑝 = 0.65,
so the null extends from held-out simulated data to the measurements themselves. The term
is genuinely being minimised, and under these supervised regimes it supplied no measurable
incremental information for any evaluated task metric. Whether it would supply information on
noisy, sparse or unlabelled data was not tested.
Chapter 5. Discussion                                                                             64



The closest published comparison reports the opposite outcome. Baniya and Maity (2026) apply
a parallel physics-informed network to damage location and severity in a shear frame and report
accurate localisation under high noise. The two studies are not directly contradictory because
they test different combinations of model, data and damage representation. Their network
consumes measured acceleration responses sampled across the structure against three scalars
from a single point here, and damage applied as a storey-level stiffness reduction lies inside the
span of a storey-parametrised model where damage applied at a plate does not. Their baseline is
also a response surface method rather than the identical network with the physics term removed
which is the pattern McGreivy and Hakim (2024) found in 79% of studies claiming to beat a
standard numerical method, surveying differential-equation solvers rather than monitoring. The
ablation here was designed against that objection, comparing the same network at 𝜆 = 0 against
two reference predictors on the same folds and it is reportable only because of the seed sweep,
the effect of the weight being smaller than the scatter that single-run comparisons hide (Lenau
et al., 2026). A staged ablation in a monitoring context did find the term helping (Al-Adly
and Kripakaran, 2024), on a forward field-reconstruction problem where the residual supplies
information the sparse data does not contain. Both stand and together they suggest the question
is whether a physics loss constrains something the supervision leaves undetermined. Only
four weights of one residual form were tested here, and loss balancing matters (Wang et al.,
2021).


5.4     What the modal model does not capture
In the moderate-base cell of Section 4.3.3, 𝑓1 scatters 85 times more than 𝑓3 so the instability is
not a property of the capture or the tap. Treated as a feature in its own right, that scatter carries
no information about severity, its rank correlation with grade being 0.004 at 𝑝 = 0.99 and it
is not even monotone, since severe sits below moderate at both the base plate and Floor 1. It
does carry information about position: classified by scatter alone under grouped leave-one-out,
location is recovered in 14 of 24 records against a chance rate of 25%, at a group permutation
𝑝 = 0.0075, and the effect is driven almost entirely by the base plate, whose median scatter of
1.967% is 9.9 times that of any storey.

None of this belongs to the linear time-invariant system the modal model assumes. A first mode
that moves 12.75% across five nominally identical taps on a fixed physical state, while the third
mode holds to 0.15%, points to an amplitude-dependent contact condition at the loosened joint
rather than to a stationary linear system observed with noise. The forward model carries no
parameter for that condition.

5.5     What the rig can and cannot stand in for
The most substantial limitation is the distance between the test structure and the building stock
the work is aimed at. Loosening a screwed joint on a bolted steel frame is a controlled, localised
and reversible change at a connection. Unreinforced masonry fails under lateral loading through
in-plane diagonal shear cracking (Hafner et al., 2023) and through out-of-plane mechanisms
whose activation depends on the strength of connections between walls, floors and roofs (Nale
et al., 2021; Destro Bisol et al., 2024). None of that is reproduced here and nothing in these
results establishes that the method transfers to masonry construction.

Independent of the choice of test structure are the two limits of Section 2.2: frequencies
respond to stiffness where strength governs collapse, and modal identification characterises the
elastic state rather than the inelastic response mobilised during strong shaking. The output of this
pipeline is therefore a change-detection signal rather than a safety assessment and its plausible role
is to prioritise the building-by-building inspection procedure of Section 2.1, not to substitute for it.

Environmental variability was not imposed. Variability of the magnitude reported on the
Z24 bridge, 14 to 18% (Peeters and De Roeck, 2001) would exceed every trace-grade shift
measured here, which run from 0.97% to 4.27% and would overlap the bottom of the severe-grade
range of 15.5 to 60.5%, which illustrates why a reassembly floor cannot be treated as a field
detection threshold. Hardware and excitation carry further constraints. The ADXL345 is a
consumer-grade part not marketed for structural monitoring, at the noisy end of the 22.5 to
              √
over 100 𝜇𝑔/ Hz range reported for devices validated in deployments (Ribeiro and Lameiras,
2019; Komarizadehasl et al., 2022) and every measurement used a controlled impulse rather
than the ambient vibration a deployed node would work from. What the campaign does establish
is that the chain from acquisition through modal identification runs on the node itself, where
published Raspberry Pi deployments have generally streamed data to a base station for processing
(Caballero-Russi et al., 2022).

5.6     Implications and further work
The comparison does not simply favour the model-free route. Each side fails somewhere
different and the difference matters for deployment. The model-based routes fail because no
parametrisation tried can represent the damage the rig applies. The nearest-signature rule
succeeds only where a library of reference signatures already exists, which on this rig meant
Chapter 5. Discussion                                                                              66

measuring every damage location in advance. No such library can be assembled for an occupied
dwelling, and freedom from one is precisely what a working forward model would buy. The
classical route is nonetheless the one to deploy on the evidence available, since it needs no
training and no retargeting for each structure and it localised correctly where the network did
not, but its reference requirement is a real constraint and not a detail.

The most useful next step follows from the failure of the plate hypothesis. Both parametrisations
tried scale a storey shear stiffness, and neither can express what loosening a screwed connection
physically does, which is to release rotational restraint at a joint rather than to soften a column in
shear. A shear-chain model has no parameter for that release, so a semi-rigid connection model,
carrying a rotational spring at each plate and a boundary parameter at the base, is the natural
next family to test against the same measured vectors. That prediction is falsifiable in the same
way the plate hypothesis was, and it uses measurements already in hand. Chapter 6 sets out that
test and three further experiments the campaign would need before any field claim.

Conclusion

One accelerometer on a three-storey shear frame, with screwed joints loosened at four locations
in four grades, supported two analysis routes. Every route that passes through a parametrised
model of the frame fails in the same place. Direct inversion closes every measured frequency
exactly and at two of the three locations where the problem is square, its solutions require
a storey to have stiffened, which loosening a screw cannot do. The one admissible solution
softens a storey the loosened plate does not adjoin. Re-parametrising by place does not repair
it: no hypothesis fits any measured case and its ranking is right 1 time in 4, which is chance.
The network inherits the storey parametrisation in full. It calls the bottom or the top storey
and almost never the middle one, so fails at Floor 2, whose plate is the one requiring the
middle storey. Its physics term cut the residual it penalises by 17.8% and moved no task
metric beyond fold and seed scatter on any dataset. Storey stiffness was recovered at no
location. The one route that applies no forward model got every replicated severe run right. The
obstacle lies in the model those routes share rather than in the identifiability of the inverse problem.

Table 5.1 records the position reached against each objective. Five teardown and rebuild
cycles put the reassembly floors at 0.30, 0.46 and 0.32%, measured rather than assumed. Damage
at a screwed joint is detectable against them: severe damage clears the first-mode floor by 52 to
202 times, and an eighth of a turn at all four locations, on the first mode at three and at Floor 3
only on the third. Detection is where the two routes agree, both classifying all five rig cases
correctly. The first-mode shift falls monotonically with grade at all four locations and the trend
holds at 11 of 12 location and mode combinations. The shift saturates above one turn and the
cells below severe are single application, so severity is an ordinal trend and not a classification.
Localisation is the strongest positive result: 12 of 12 replicated severe runs correct, and 18 of 18
at two other sensor positions against references recorded elsewhere. All of this came from a
consumer-grade part at the noisy end of the published range, with the whole chain running on

Chapter 6. Conclusion                                                                             68

the node rather than a base station.

The research question asked whether embedding the equation of motion in a learned in-
verse model improves on classical modal analysis. It does not. The classical route is the one to
put on a low-cost node: it returns 9 of 9 where the network returns 6 of 9, and needs no forward
model and no retargeting per structure. The comparison is not one-sided. Nearest signature
works only against a library of reference signatures, which on this rig meant measuring every
damage location in advance, and no occupied dwelling would permit that. A working forward
model would buy freedom from that library.

The rig is a bolted steel frame and nothing here establishes transfer to unreinforced ma-
sonry, whose failure is governed by strength rather than the stiffness a frequency observes. No
second structure was measured. Five rebuild cycles give an operational floor rather than a formal
exclusion limit, and no thermal variation was imposed, so the floor bounds reassembly and not
deployment. Under these supervised synthetic settings, the physics term supplied no measurable
information beyond the labels. The trained weights behind the rig predictions were not retained,
one of those predictions rests on imputed modes so its localisation does not stand, and the healthy
case is not an independent test. The output is a change-detection signal. It could prioritise
inspection; it is not a safety assessment.

Four experiments follow. The first needs no new measurements. Both parametrisations
tried scale a storey shear stiffness and neither expresses what loosening a screwed connection
does, which is to release rotational restraint at a joint. A model carrying a rotational spring
at each plate and boundary parameter at the base can be fitted to the vectors already recorded
and tested for admissibility. A solution identifies the release the shear chain cannot represent;
its absence moves the discrepancy off the connection. Varying temperature at fixed damage
replaces the reassembly floor with an environmental one, against which trace-grade margin
here must be rejudged. Operational modal analysis would establish whether the identifica-
tion chain survives ambient excitation, since no dwelling supplies a controlled impulse. The
fourth returns to the physics term, whose null rests on labelled synthetic data alone; repeating
the ablation on unlabelled or heavily noised captures would test it where it was designed to operate.

A physics term earns its place where it constrains something the supervision leaves unde-
termined, and here the labels and the inputs came through the same forward map the residual
inverts. That bounds when such a term helps, not whether it ever does. In this controlled
campaign, the limit on stiffness recovery came from the structural model rather than measurement
resolution.
